The honest answer to "how much does a mobile app cost?" is "it depends on what you build." Two apps that look similar on a phone can need very different amounts of work behind the screen. Anyone who quotes a price without asking questions is guessing.

You can still plan. Costs follow a handful of drivers, and once you know them you can shape the project to fit a budget. This guide explains the main drivers, the costs that appear after launch and how to get quotes you can compare.

## Why there is no single price

An app is a set of decisions: what it does, on which devices, how it looks, what it connects to and how it is kept running. Each decision adds or removes work. A simple app that displays information needs far less effort than one with accounts, payments, live data and an admin dashboard.

Rather than asking for a price, ask for an estimate for a clearly defined scope. The rest of this guide helps you define it.

## The main cost drivers

### 1. Features and complexity

Every feature costs design, development, testing and maintenance. Features that look small can hide a lot of work. "Users can log in" includes sign-up, password reset, error states, security and account deletion.

### 2. Platforms

Building for iOS, Android or both changes the scope. Cross-platform tools let one codebase serve both and can reduce effort. Native development for each platform gives the most control over performance and device features but takes more work. Your audience's devices should guide the choice. Our comparison of [mobile app vs web app](/blog/mobile-app-vs-web-app-which-to-build-first) covers the decision from another angle.

### 3. Design depth

A clean interface built from standard components costs less than custom illustrations, animations and a bespoke design system. Good design is not optional, because it affects whether people finish the main task, but you can choose how far to take it in version one.

### 4. Backend and integrations

Most apps need a server side: a database, user accounts, file storage and an admin area. Integrations with payment providers, maps, calendars, messaging or other software add work and ongoing fees. Each outside service also has its own rules and failure modes to handle.

### 5. AI features

AI features add design, data handling, testing and running costs, since model usage is billed by volume. They can be worth it when they save users real effort. See [AI features for mobile apps](/blog/ai-features-for-mobile-apps) for how to judge that.

### 6. Security, privacy and compliance

Apps that handle payments, health information or children's data carry extra requirements. Meeting them takes planning and testing, and it is far cheaper to design for them from the start than to add them later.

### 7. Team and location

Rates differ between freelancers, agencies and in-house teams, and between regions. A cheaper rate does not always mean a cheaper project if it leads to rework. Look at experience with projects like yours, and at how clearly the team communicates.

### 8. Timeline

Tight deadlines often need more people working in parallel, which raises cost. A realistic schedule with a clear scope is usually the most economical.

## Costs that appear after launch

The build is not the whole budget. Plan for:

- **Store fees.** Apple and Google charge developer account fees.
- **Hosting and services.** Servers, databases, messaging and analytics tools bill monthly.
- **Maintenance.** Operating systems and devices change every year, and libraries need updates and security fixes.
- **Support and improvements.** Real users will find problems and ask for features.
- **Marketing.** An app does not find users on its own. See [app store optimization for niche apps](/blog/app-store-optimization-for-niche-apps).

A common planning habit is to set aside a share of the build budget every year for upkeep. Ask any team you work with what their maintenance arrangement looks like.

## How to keep the cost under control

1. **Build a first version that does one job.** Cut every feature that is not needed for the main task. [How to build a niche mobile app](/blog/how-to-build-a-niche-mobile-app) shows how to choose that job.
2. **Do discovery first.** A short planning phase of research, wireframes and a feature list turns a vague idea into a scope you can price.
3. **Work in phases.** Launch, learn from users and fund the next phase from what you learned.
4. **Reuse proven components.** Standard login, payment and notification services are cheaper and more reliable than custom builds.
5. **Decide what can wait.** An admin dashboard can start simple. Advanced analytics and extra languages can come later.

## How to get quotes you can compare

Send every team the same brief. Include:

- The audience and the problem the app solves
- The core features, in priority order
- Target platforms
- Integrations you already know you need
- Your timeline and budget range
- What "done" means for version one

Then compare quotes on what they include, not only the total. Check who handles design, testing, project management and launch, and what happens after delivery.

## Red flags

- A fixed price with no questions about your goals
- No written scope or list of what is excluded
- No mention of testing, security or maintenance
- Pressure to decide quickly
- Reluctance to show similar work or explain their process

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a cheaper app a bad idea?

Not necessarily. A small, focused app can be a smart first step. The risk is paying for a low price up front and then rebuilding because the foundation was weak.

### Can I start with a prototype?

Yes. A clickable prototype lets you test the idea with real users for a small fraction of the cost of a full build.

### Does a niche app cost less than a mass-market app?

Often the scope is smaller, because you serve one audience's main needs. The effort still depends on features and integrations, not on the size of the audience.
