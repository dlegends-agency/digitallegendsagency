Most app ideas fail because nobody checked whether people wanted them, not because the code was bad. Niche market research is how you check. It takes days or weeks, costs very little compared with development and can save months of building the wrong thing.

This guide covers the research we run before we design a niche app: how to define the audience, where to find them, what to ask, how to read competitor reviews and how to decide whether to move forward.

## Why research comes before design

When your audience is small, a wrong guess is expensive. You cannot recover from a poor launch by sending more traffic, because there is only so much traffic in a niche. Research reduces the guessing. It shows you which problems are painful enough that people pay to solve them, and which are mild annoyances nobody will download an app for.

It also gives you the language of your audience. The words people use to describe their problem become your app store listing, your onboarding text and your website copy.

## Step 1: Define the niche precisely

Write down who the user is, what they do and in what situation the problem appears. "Small business owners" is too broad. "Owners of single-location bakeries who take custom orders by message" is a niche.

Add boundaries: where they live or work, how technical they are, what devices they use. These details decide where you recruit people for interviews and which channels you can use later.

## Step 2: Find where your audience talks

People in a niche gather in predictable places:

- Online forums and subreddits about the activity or profession
- Facebook, LinkedIn and Discord groups
- Trade associations, newsletters and podcasts
- Review sections of the tools they already use
- Local meetups, courses and trade shows

Read before you post. Look for repeated complaints, workarounds people share and questions that come up again and again. Save the exact phrases. Patterns across many threads are stronger evidence than one loud comment.

## Step 3: Interview five to ten people

Interviews give you the depth that surveys miss. Aim for five to ten conversations of about thirty minutes with people who match your niche definition.

Ask about the past, not the future:

- "Tell me about the last time you had to [do the task]. What happened?"
- "What tools did you use? What did you do when they didn't work?"
- "What is the most annoying part of this?"
- "Have you tried to fix it? What did you try?"
- "What does it cost you in time or money when it goes wrong?"

Avoid pitching your idea in the first half of the call. People are polite and will say a hypothetical app sounds great. What they did last week is more reliable than what they say they would do.

## Step 4: Study competitors and their reviews

List every tool your audience uses for the problem, including spreadsheets, paper and general apps. Then read the one- and two-star reviews of the closest apps. They show what frustrates users in their own words.

Look for:

- Features people ask for that no app provides
- Complaints about complexity, price or missing support
- Workflows the apps force on users that do not match real work

Gaps that appear across many reviews point to a product opportunity. Note that a crowded category is not always bad news. It proves demand. The question is whether anyone serves your specific niche well.

## Step 5: Check whether the niche can support a product

You do not need a precise market size. You need an honest answer to three questions:

1. **Can you reach these people?** You should be able to name three places to find them.
2. **Is the problem frequent or costly?** Daily irritation and expensive mistakes justify a product. Rare inconveniences do not.
3. **Do they already pay for solutions?** People who pay for tools, services or workarounds will usually pay for a better one.

A simple test is a landing page that describes the app and collects email addresses. If you can drive a small group from your niche to sign up, you have a signal.

## Step 6: Turn findings into decisions

Summarize your research on one page:

- The audience in one sentence
- The top three problems, in the audience's words
- How they solve each problem today
- The one problem your first version will solve
- What would make you stop

That last item matters. Decide in advance what evidence would convince you the idea is not worth building. It protects you from falling in love with a weak idea.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many interviews are enough?

Most founders hear the same problems repeated after five to eight conversations. Keep going until new interviews stop surprising you.

### What if I cannot find anyone to interview?

That is also research. If you cannot find or reach people in the niche, marketing the app later will be just as hard. Try a different angle on the audience or a channel where they gather.

### Should I use surveys instead?

Surveys work well after interviews, when you know what to ask and want to measure how common a problem is. They are weak for discovering problems you have not thought of.
